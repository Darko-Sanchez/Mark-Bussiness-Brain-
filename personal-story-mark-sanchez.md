## SECTION 1: ORIGIN & FOUNDATION

### The Moment That Changed Everything

I've always been the go-to person. My schedule was always six or seven days a week, working doubles. I was like the fucking workhorse, and it annoyed the shit out of me at a moment in my life because I felt like, "Damn, why am I constantly working?" I would be first in and last out, and I thought it was because I was so good at what I did that people just wanted to keep me there.

Then there was a time I was like, "I don't want to work as much anymore." I started creating experiences instead of working for money. I wanted to go down to three days a week versus six, but I was so scared. One day I put it on the owner — I want to go down to three days. To my surprise, she was like, "OK, not a problem, done."

I was like, "Wow, it was that easy. All I had to do was ask." In my mind, they were using and abusing me, but it was just because I never asked. That shaped how I started moving through the rest of my life. If you don't ask, the answer is always no. If you don't speak up, someone is always gonna be running you.

---

### The Person Who Shaped Them

My mom. She always told me I could do whatever it is I want to do — all my life. Throughout my life, all the things that I did, the good and the bad, she always supported me. She never judged me. She would disagree with me at times, but no matter what, she would always say, "You made your decisions, and I'm going to roll with you."

She never tried to force her religion on me. She never tried to force her ideas on me. Even when I was dealing drugs, she didn't want me to — but because I chose that life, she didn't stop me either. She mentored me and tried to steer me away from it, but because she stood with me regardless, I was like: if my mom could have these types of thoughts and ideas around me, everybody else can also.

---

### The Value Running Their Decisions

Acknowledging people in the room. Even if I don't like the person, I still show up, I still acknowledge them, and I still treat them with respect. Even though sometimes I don't want to, it's just ingrained in me. Just because you don't like somebody doesn't mean you can't work with them. We're here to get a job done, and that's what we're going to do.

My parents instilled in us that we have to shake everybody's hand, give everybody a hug, tell everybody hi, tell everybody bye. I do that now — I walk into work, I acknowledge everybody. When I leave, I acknowledge everybody. That's just the way it is with me when I enter a room.

The other thing that stuck: always offering to help. No matter what, I could be at the grocery store and see somebody needing help, and I just want to help — even when I'm busy.

## SECTION 2: CHARACTER UNDER PRESSURE

### The Hardest Chapter

I was about 33 years old. Living at my aunt's house. Drinking every night. Working six, seven days a week. Broke. Fat. Not even paying rent. I felt like a loser.

Stuck in this cycle of working hard, playing harder, coming home, drinking every single day, sleeping with random women, going out with people I thought were my friends, buying drinks for everybody — being this person I thought I needed to be so people would like me. I kept myself busy so I didn't have to sit with myself. I kept myself drunk so I didn't have to sit with my emotions.

That felt like shit. I felt depressed, sad, alone — and I thought it was the world doing this to me. For a moment it felt like I was just gonna be like this forever. I'm gonna end up back on my mom's couch, back home in Wapato, just like everybody else. I hated myself for that.

---

### The Risk That Defined Them

Two moments stand out.

First: I used to be scared of traveling. I thought I'd get kidnapped in another country, have my organs harvested. In 2017, I met a group of people and went to this talk — a lady was talking about Peru. I was intrigued because she was showing pictures of places I'd only seen on Ancient Aliens. I felt this draw.

At the end of her talk I asked how much. Seven thousand dollars — which I had nowhere near. I said, "Can I put $500 down?" She said yes, with this look on her face like, "How are you going to pull this off?" I was even like, "How am I going to pull this off?" My mom was worried. My friends were worried. They said, "You're crazy." I thought I was crazy too.

But on the other side of that, it opened me up to so much more. Holy shit — I like to travel. I like to see new things. I like to connect with people from all walks of life. It was my first real interaction with multicultural people from different countries at a different level. That opened me up for my next level.

Second: leaving my job after 12 years. I was there through thick and thin, blood, sweat, and tears. I grew there, found myself there, lost myself there — all of the above. Last year I decided my values were no longer aligned with theirs and they weren't looking to move forward. I didn't know what I was going to do. I was scared. What am I going to do about money?

I quit. I went to travel Europe and Asia for four or five months. I got connected with other coaches who'd quit six-figure and seven-figure jobs to follow their dreams. When I got back I was like: okay, I'm bored. Let's put what I've learned into practice. That trip helped develop me into the person I am today.

---

### The Mistake That Built Them

In eighth grade, I got a gun at school. We were gang banging and selling drugs — gangsters need guns. I got caught. Somebody snitched, I got kicked out of school, and I got sent to Arizona. My dad sent me away. I felt like he didn't want me.

That decision cost me my friends. It cost me my family. It cost me my emotional state — because my mom went to prison at the same time. I'm not wanted. I can't even see my friends. I had to go build a whole other life. I lost everything, basically.

But it taught me to be more careful in the way I operate. More diligent in who I connect with, the things I say, the things I don't say. Stop trusting someone who's really just an acquaintance with something that matters. Moving forward, I learned to be more discerning about who I share things with and how much of myself I give.

It also opened me up to being able to move easily — to get up and go. I have no problem leaving anymore. The world is so much bigger. I don't have to be stuck and trapped by friends or family. I can always come back to them.

---

### Standing Their Ground

Around 2005-2006, I was working for a company called Azteca — my first job in the hospitality industry. We moved to Florida to open a store. We were being taken advantage of by the GM, the owner. We were paying $500 a month for a room in his house when the whole mortgage was probably $600.

One day he started accusing everyone of stealing his phone, which he'd actually just misplaced. Accusing the workers was one thing. But starting to accuse me and my brother and the people who'd come to help build this thing — unacceptable.

Me and my brother decided: we're not cleaning, we're not staying, fuck you, we're leaving. He wanted a meeting. He said his part. I said mine. I didn't appreciate how you came about it.

He offered to let me come back if I signed paperwork saying I'd gone against his authority. I said: no, fuck you, I ain't signing shit. I don't believe in what you did and I don't believe in what you're doing.

It cost me. My brother had to leave. I had to leave. I'd built a whole life there — furniture, my car, friends, everything. We just got up and left. But if I'd allowed him to take advantage of me, I would have been under his thumb forever. It lost me the partnership, the friendship, the life I'd built in Florida. That was the right thing to do.

## SECTION 3: THE WORK & THE WHY

### When They Knew

Around 2017. I'd just had my health under control for a while, getting people into nutritional and cleanse systems. I went to work at On the Border in Las Vegas. I met a coworker who talked about wanting to get her life in shape, stop drinking, get her body right.

I mentioned what I did. We talked in passing a few times. One day she walked up to me: "I wanna know a little bit more about what you do and how you do it." This was before I had any formal training. I said, "If you wanna meet me at the gym, let's go have a session and just talk, and we'll see where we go from there."

That was the moment. Holy shit — people hear the words I'm saying, and they're curious. Watching her transformation is what really helped me understand this could work. She went from being a total alcoholic, drinking all the time, being overweight — to getting her health under control, stopping drinking so much, becoming a better person.

---

### What They Do Differently

I'm actually in the restaurants. I got the proof, I got the case studies. I get to know not just the workers but the actual clientele.

I also take a no-bullshit approach — either we're doing this or we're not. There's no in between. I don't coddle you. I'm not your mom, I'm not your dad. I'm here to help you get results.

And I can take them through the steps in real time, on the floor with real people, so they can see it in action. I can sit in the restaurant and watch them move through a business night. Not to mention, they get to see the social proof with the Google reviews I got in two different places, two different restaurants.

---

### The Reframe That Changes Everything

Your outside world is the reflection of your inside world. You're the reason why you're upset. You're the reason why you feel shitty. And it's helping people recognize the chaos that's inside them, reflect on why they're having it, then redirect it. They're like, "Wow, I never realized that person is reflecting a part of me back."

That's why I love using movies so much — people who don't know how to connect to their emotions can watch a movie and feel something. I help them see that the emotion they're feeling in the movie is tied to something inside them that's happening in real life. It's easier to talk about a character than about yourself. But once they realize they're actually talking about themselves — "Oh shit, I never saw it like that."

Same principle in a restaurant: when a guest is annoying you because they're asking a million questions about a dish, the question isn't why are they being annoying — it's "where do you find yourself asking these types of questions in your own life?" They're like, "Oh shit, I never looked at it like that."

---

### The Work That Made Them Proud

I was working with a kid whose mom owned the last restaurant I worked for. He didn't want to get stuck in the business. He had no boundaries, couldn't tell his mom no, and wasn't taking the actions he said he was going to take. He was working six, seven days a week across two stores and didn't know how to say no.

Once we started working together, he had no idea what he wanted to do. He just knew he didn't want to work in a restaurant. We put together a plan — a list of possibilities: electrical, air conditioning, possibly the power company APS.

Little by little, he started reaching out, applying for jobs. He got a job he didn't fully want, but it was in the field he wanted. He stuck with it for six months, still working part time at the restaurant. He totally got out — then came back part time because he needed the money while doing both simultaneously. Then he landed his dream job with APS, which doesn't come around very often.

He also built a relationship with the girl he'd been dating and they became boyfriend and girlfriend. Because he could take the advice, do the work, and become the person he wanted to become — he broke away and got what he wanted.

## SECTION 4: WORLDVIEW & DIRECTION

### What Everyone Gets Wrong

"Do as I say, not as I do." People don't lead by example, and that annoys the fuck out of me. How are you gonna tell people what to do when you don't even do it? I see it in coaches. I see it in training. I see it in their day-to-day lives. I know some of these people — they're my peers — and I'm like, "How do people come to you when you're not even following the shit that you talk about?"

---

### How Success Changed Shape

I used to think success was having a good job, making lots of money. I thought I was successful when I was in a management position. It was about working hard, going to the grind, pleasing other people.

Now? Success is waking up and not dreading the day. My body feels good. I don't dread getting on the computer. I don't dread getting up at 1:00 AM to learn this AI stuff so I can move forward and better my life.

Success looks like having great conversations with people I love. Opening up to relationships — romantic relationships — and not being scared of that. Even though I don't make as much money as I used to when I was selling drugs or managing restaurants, my life is a lot calmer. A lot easier. I do a lot more with less.

What changed my mind: reading the books, taking the courses, doing the inner work. I was chasing somebody else's dream, not mine. Having the house, the cars, the job — that's what society wanted me to do. That's why my life was so chaotic, so emotionally charged. I was anxious all the time. Depressed all the time. Drinking all the time. Because I wasn't following the path for me.

---

### Where This Is All Going

The industry is heading into more automation, more robotics, more AI. People are going to start relying too much on AI tools — and also not embracing them enough. The people I'm working with are going to have to utilize the best of both worlds: use AI and automation for the parts that aren't customer-facing, and really embrace emotional intelligence and the guest experience for the human side.

The two are going to have to work together. If you only rely on one side, you lose out. If you don't adopt either or learn how to put them together to get the best outcome, you get left behind. The people who embrace the changes, utilize AI in a way that's helpful, and integrate the emotional aspect — those are the people who are going to win.

---

### What People Actually Get

When they're done with me, they feel more confident. They connect better and easier. They handle stress with ease and grace. They become responsive versus reactive. They show up to work like, "Wow, this is amazing" — or they leave their job because they realize it's not for them. They become more clear on what they want in life and how to get it.

The restaurants that implement what I'm doing realize: we've had it wrong this whole time. It's not about the transaction. It's about how we get the guest to feel something — because if we can get them to feel something amazing, the money follows. People tell other people. The loop continues. We stop treating people like numbers and start treating them like human beings.
